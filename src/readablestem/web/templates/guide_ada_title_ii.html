ADA Title II and STEM course materials

Last updated {{ updated_text }}. By Harish Manoharan. The rules and dates below were checked against the sources at the bottom of the page on 28 October 2026.

The Department of Justice's ADA Title II web rule asks public universities to make their online course materials accessible to students with disabilities. This page says what the rule requires, when, and what that means for STEM materials. It is guidance, not legal advice.

What the rule requires

The rule is 28 CFR part 35, subpart H. The Department of Justice published it in the Federal Register on April 24, 2024. It covers the web content and mobile apps that a public entity provides or makes available, directly or through contractual, licensing, or other arrangements (28 CFR 35.200(a)).

The technical standard is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) version 2.1, Level A and Level AA (28 CFR 35.200(b)). A public entity does not have to meet it where it can show that doing so would fundamentally alter a service, program or activity, or cause undue financial and administrative burdens.

When it applies

The 2024 rule set April 24, 2026 and April 26, 2027. On April 20, 2026 the Department published an interim final rule (91 FR 20902) that moved each date by one year. It took effect that day, and comments were due by June 22, 2026.

As of October 28, 2026 we found nothing newer. The Federal Register lists no later document that changes 28 CFR part 35, and the current eCFR text of section 35.200 is the April 20, 2026 version. But the dates may still change. The interim final rule says the Department plans future rulemaking on the substance of the 2024 rule, and will consider proposing a rule during the extension. If it does not, and nothing suggests a further delay, it “fully anticipates implementing the regulation at the new deadline.” Check ADA.gov before you rely on a date.

Which date applies to a university depends on its total population as 28 CFR 35.104 defines it. For a public entity that has no Census population estimate of its own and is an instrumentality of a State or local government, the population of that government counts. Your campus accessibility office can say which date applies to you.

The dates are not the start of the duty. The interim final rule says that, whatever the compliance dates, public entities have an ongoing obligation to make the services, programs and activities they offer on the web accessible under their existing obligations under title II of the ADA.

What it means for STEM course materials

Course materials put online are web content. The rule names PDF, word processor, presentation and spreadsheet files as “conventional electronic documents” and lists them as web content (28 CFR 35.104).

In 2023 the Department proposed an exception for password-protected course content. The final rule leaves it out (28 CFR 35.201 has no such exception), so a course file behind a login is not excepted for that reason.

One exception may matter. Conventional electronic documents that were available before the date a public entity must comply are excepted, unless they are currently used to apply for, gain access to, or participate in its services, programs, or activities (28 CFR 35.201(b)). The Department's fact sheet gives an example of a document that loses the exception once it is updated. Whether a given course file falls under the exception is for your campus to decide. This page does not decide it.

Where STEM files usually fall short

These are the parts of WCAG 2.1 that STEM files meet most often. The list is not complete.

  • Equations. An equation that is a picture needs a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose (success criterion 1.1.1). An equation written as MathML is text, so a screen reader reads it aloud, for example “v equals d over t”, and lets students explore it.
  • Figures. The same criterion: a figure needs a text alternative. A chart or a diagram may need a longer description than a short alt text.
  • Tables. The relationships between cells and their headers have to be in the code (success criterion 1.3.1). Without them, screen readers stop saying which row and column a value belongs to.
  • Headings. Headings have to be real headings, not larger bold text (success criterion 1.3.1), so that students can move through the document by them.
  • Scanned pages and handwriting. A scanned page is a picture of text. WCAG 2.1 asks for text rather than images of text (success criterion 1.4.5, with exceptions), so the text has to be recovered, and the math checked. A tool can misread handwriting and small symbols.

What ReadableSTEM does and does not do

ReadableSTEM assists instructors who need to turn a STEM course PDF into an accessible web page. It reads the PDF, rebuilds equations as MathML a screen reader can speak, restores headings and lists, writes short alt text for figures, and returns one self-contained HTML file. Every change it is unsure about goes to a review screen where the instructor accepts, edits or rejects it.

It assists; it does not certify. Nothing here establishes conformance with WCAG, the ADA or any other standard, and the output still needs a person to look at it. That is what the review screen is for.

  • It is not a compliance checker. The checks look at structure, not meaning, and nothing here replaces testing with a screen reader.
  • It makes mistakes, so look over the equations your students rely on. How accurate it is and the known limits, including scanned and handwritten pages.
  • It starts from a PDF.

The rule allows a separate accessible version of a page only where it is not possible to make the web content directly accessible because of technical or legal limitations (28 CFR 35.202(a)). So post the HTML as the main course material, not as an extra beside an inaccessible PDF. How to post it in your course, and an example conversion to look at first.
