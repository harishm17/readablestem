CHAPTER 5

Population Growth

Every population changes in size over time. Individuals are born, individuals die, and some move in or out. The balance of these four processes decides whether a population grows, shrinks or stays steady.1
Immigration and emigration are the movements of individuals into and out of a population.

5.1 Exponential Growth

When resources are unlimited, a population grows by a constant fraction each time period. The population after t periods is given by the equation below, where N0 is the starting size and r is the growth rate per period.
Bacteria in a fresh flask of nutrient broth are a classic example of nearly exponential growth.

Key idea

Exponential growth looks slow at first and then very fast. A population that doubles every day is 1,024 times larger after ten days.
Real populations cannot grow this way for long. Food, space and disease eventually slow the growth, which is why most populations show a different pattern.
Thomas Malthus noticed this tension in 1798.

5.2 Logistic Growth

In the logistic model, growth slows as the population approaches the carrying capacity K. The growth rate falls to zero when N reaches K, and the population levels off at that size.2
Carrying capacity is the largest population the environment can support for a long time.

Example 5.1

A pond can support 500 fish. If 50 fish are introduced and the growth rate is 0.4 per month, the population grows quickly at first and approaches 500 after about a year.
Ecologists test the logistic model by comparing its curve with counts from real populations, such as deer on an island or yeast in a flask.
1 Ecologists sometimes call births and deaths the natural increase of a population.
2 The logistic model was first proposed by Pierre Verhulst in 1838.
