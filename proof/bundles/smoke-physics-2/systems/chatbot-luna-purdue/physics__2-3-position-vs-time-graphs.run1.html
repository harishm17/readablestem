2.3 Position vs. Time Graphs

Section Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • Explain the meaning of slope in position-versus-time graphs.
  • Solve problems using position-versus-time graphs.

The student is expected to generate and interpret graphs and charts describing different types of motion, including the use of real-time technology such as motion detectors or photogates.

Teacher Support: Introducing the Topic

Describe a scenario such as launching a water rocket into the air. It rises 150 ft, stops, and then falls back to Earth. Ask students where they would put zero, which direction is positive, and which is negative. Have a student draw the scenario, then work together to draw a position-versus-time graph. Ask whether the line is straight or curved, whether it changes direction, and what can be learned from the graph.

After students analyze the graph, ask them to describe scenarios in which the graph would be straight rather than curved, or discontinuous.

Graphing Position as a Function of Time

A graph, like a picture, is worth a thousand words. Graphs contain numerical information and reveal relationships between physical quantities. In this section, we investigate kinematics by analyzing graphs of position over time.

Graphs have perpendicular axes: one horizontal and one vertical. When two physical quantities are plotted against each other, the horizontal axis is usually the independent variable, and the vertical axis is the dependent variable. In algebra, these are the \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis. A straight-line graph has the general form:

\[ y = mx + b \]

Here, \(m\) is the slope, defined as rise divided by run. The letter \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept: the point at which the line crosses the vertical \(y\)-axis.

In physics, time is usually the independent variable. Other quantities, such as displacement, depend on time. Therefore, a position-versus-time graph has position on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. What do the slope and the \(y\)-intercept mean in this situation?

Suppose the drive to school is 5 km from home, takes 10 minutes, and your parent drives at constant velocity the whole time. The position-versus-time graph for this part of the trip is shown below.

Home is chosen as the origin, so the initial position is \(d_0=0\). The line starts at \(d=0\), which is the intercept \(b\) in the straight-line equation. The initial position on a position-versus-time graph is the position at \(t=0\). The rise is the change in position (displacement), and the run is the change in time:

\[ \text{slope} = \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} \]

This is the definition of average velocity. Therefore, the slope of a \(d\)-versus-\(t\) graph is average velocity.

Tips for Success

A graph of a trip to school and the return trip may have different behavior during different time intervals. If the graph is a series of straight lines, calculate the average velocity for each interval from its slope. To calculate the average velocity for the entire trip, use a weighted average.

Figure 2.12 shows a position-versus-time graph for a jet-powered car on a very flat dry lake bed in Nevada.

The slope in Figure 2.12 is average velocity, \(v_{\text{avg}}\), and the intercept is the displacement at time zero, \(d_0\). Substituting these symbols into \(y=mx+b\) gives:

\[ d = vt + d_0 \] \[ d = d_0 + vt \]

A position-versus-time graph therefore gives a general relationship among displacement, velocity, and time, as well as numerical information about a particular situation. From the graph, the car is at 400 m when \(t=0\text{ s}\), and at 650 m when \(t=1.0\text{ s}\).

Teacher Support: Demonstration

Help students learn what different displacement-versus-time graphs look like.

Set up a meter stick. If possible, use a remote-control car:

  1. Have one student record times as you move the car forward along the stick, then backward, then forward again at constant velocity.
  2. Combine the recorded times with the changes in position.
  3. Have students coach you as you draw a position-versus-time graph.

Each leg of the journey should be a straight line with a different slope. Forward motion has a positive slope; backward motion has a negative slope.

  • Ask whether the choice of zero affects the graph.
  • Ask whether a position graph for an object that starts at rest should include a curve, and why a curve might be neglected in some situations.
  • Discuss what can be learned from the graph. Students can read net displacement and determine total distance traveled. The steepness of the line (slope) measures speed, and the direction of the slope indicates direction of motion.
  • A curve represents a changing slope, previewing acceleration.

Snap Lab: Graphing Motion

In this activity, release a ball down a ramp and graph the ball’s displacement versus time.

Choose an open location with plenty of space to reduce the chance of tripping or falling because of rolling balls.

Materials

  • 1 ball
  • 1 board
  • 2 or 3 books
  • 1 stopwatch
  • 1 tape measure
  • 6 pieces of masking tape
  • 1 piece of graph paper
  • 1 pencil

Procedure

  1. Build a ramp by placing one end of the board on the stack of books. Adjust the location until there is a clear, straight path from the bottom of the ramp for at least 3 m.
  2. Mark distances of 0.5 m, 1.0 m, 1.5 m, 2.0 m, 2.5 m, and 3.0 m from the bottom of the ramp. Write the distances on the tape.
  3. One person releases the ball from the top of the ramp. If the ball does not reach the 3.0 m mark, increase the ramp’s incline by adding another book. Repeat as necessary.
  4. The experimenter releases the ball. A second person starts timing when the ball reaches the bottom of the ramp and stops timing when it reaches 0.5 m. A third person records the time in a data table.
  5. Repeat the timing, stopping at 1.0 m, 1.5 m, 2.0 m, 2.5 m, and 3.0 m.
  6. Use the measured times and displacements to make a position-versus-time graph of the ball’s motion.
  7. Repeat the measurements with different people acting as experimenter, timer, and recorder. Compare the results and discuss possible causes of discrepancies.

True or false: The average speed of the ball will be less than the average velocity of the ball.

  1. True
  2. False

Teacher Support: Snap Lab

  • Emphasize that this lab concerns the ball’s motion as it rolls along the floor. Ask students where the zero should be.
  • Ask how the graph would differ if timing began at the top rather than the bottom of the ramp, and what might account for the difference.
  • Have students compare graphs made by different people. Ask them to calculate and compare average speeds for each interval, including absolute and percent differences. Discuss whether differences are random or systematic and why.

Solving Problems Using Position-versus-Time Graphs

How can we use graphs to solve for quantities such as velocity?

Worked Example: Calculating Average Velocity for a Jet Car

Find the average velocity of the car whose position is graphed in Figure 2.12.

Strategy

The slope of a graph of \(d\) versus \(t\) is average velocity because slope equals rise over run:

\[ \text{slope} = \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} = v \]

Since the slope is constant, any two points on the line can be used.

Solution

  1. Choose two points on the line: \((6.4\text{ s}, 2000\text{ m})\) and \((0.50\text{ s}, 525\text{ m})\). Any two points on the line may be used.
  2. Substitute the position and time values. When calculating a change, use final value minus initial value:
    \[ \begin{aligned} v &= \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} \\ &= \frac{2000\text{ m}-525\text{ m}} {6.4\text{ s}-0.50\text{ s}} \\ &= 250\text{ m/s} \end{aligned} \]

Discussion

This is an impressively high land speed: 900 km/h, or about 560 mi/h. It is much greater than the typical highway speed limit of 27 m/s (96 km/h), but below the 343 m/s (1,234 km/h) record set in 1997.

Teacher Support

For a straight-line position graph, students need the slope of the line—rise divided by run—to calculate average velocity. They can use whichever points on the line are most convenient.

What if the position graph is more complicated than a straight line? What if the object speeds up or turns around and moves backward? A graph can still provide information about velocity.

Figure 2.13 shows a jet-powered car speeding up after starting at rest. Time starts at zero, as if measured with a stopwatch. The car’s initial displacement and velocity are 200 m and 15 m/s, respectively.

The position-versus-time graph in Figure 2.13 is curved rather than straight. Its slope becomes steeper as time progresses, showing that velocity increases over time. The slope at any point on a position-versus-time graph is the instantaneous velocity at that point. Find it by drawing a straight line tangent to the curve at the point of interest and calculating the tangent’s slope. Average velocity is net displacement divided by elapsed time.

Worked Example: Instantaneous Velocity for a Jet Car

Calculate the instantaneous velocity of the jet car at \(t=25\text{ s}\) by finding the slope of the tangent line at point Q in Figure 2.13.

Strategy

The slope of a curve at a point equals the slope of the straight line tangent to the curve at that point.

Solution

  1. Find the tangent line to the curve at \(t=25\text{ s}\).
  2. The tangent endpoints correspond to a position of 1,300 m at \(t=19\text{ s}\) and a position of 3,120 m at \(t=32\text{ s}\).
  3. Substitute the endpoint values to find the slope:
    \[ \begin{aligned} v_Q &= \frac{\Delta d_Q}{\Delta t_Q} \\ &= \frac{(3120-1300)\text{ m}}{(32-19)\text{ s}} \\ &= \frac{1820\text{ m}}{13\text{ s}} \\ &= 140\text{ m/s} \end{aligned} \]

Discussion

The entire graph of velocity versus time can be obtained in this way.

Teacher Support: Tangents

Define a tangent as a line that touches a curve at one point. The slope of the tangent gives instantaneous velocity. Students can use it to tell when velocity is increasing, decreasing, positive, negative, or zero. At this level, the tangent can be estimated visually.

Finding the instantaneous velocity at points along a position graph and graphing those values produces a velocity graph.
