Speed

There is more to motion than distance and displacement. Questions such as, “How long does a foot race take?” and “What was the runner’s speed?” cannot be answered without an understanding of other concepts. In this section we will look at time, speed, and velocity to expand our understanding of motion.

A description of how fast or slow an object moves is its speed. Speed is the rate at which an object changes its location. Like distance, speed is a scalar because it has a magnitude but not a direction. Because speed is a rate, it depends on the time interval of motion. You can calculate the elapsed time, or the change in time, \(\Delta t\), as the difference between the ending time and the beginning time:

\[ \Delta t = t_f - t_0 \]

The SI unit of time is the second (s), and the SI unit of speed is meters per second (m/s), but sometimes kilometers per hour (km/h), miles per hour (mph), or other units of speed are used.

When you describe an object’s speed, you often describe the average over a time period. Average speed, \(v_{\mathrm{avg}}\), is the distance traveled divided by the time during which the motion occurs.

\[ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \]

You can rearrange the equation to solve for either distance or time:

\[ \text{time} = \frac{\text{distance}}{v_{\mathrm{avg}}} \] \[ \text{distance} = v_{\mathrm{avg}} \times \text{time} \]

Suppose, for example, a car travels 150 kilometers in 3.2 hours. Its average speed for the trip is:

\[ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \frac{150\ \text{km}}{3.2\ \text{h}} = 47\ \text{km/h}. \]

A car’s speed would likely increase and decrease many times over a 3.2-hour trip. Its speed at a specific instant in time, however, is its instantaneous speed. A car’s speedometer describes its instantaneous speed.

Teacher Support

Caution students that average speed is not always the average of an object’s initial and final speeds. For example, suppose a car travels a distance of 100 km. The first 50 km it travels at 30 km/h and the second 50 km at 60 km/h. Its average speed would be \[ \frac{100\ \text{km}} {(50\ \text{km})/(30\ \text{km/h}) + (50\ \text{km})/(60\ \text{km/h})} = 40\ \text{km/h}. \] If the car had spent equal times at 30 km/h and 60 km/h rather than traveling equal distances at these speeds, its average speed would have been 45 km/h.

Caution students that the terms speed, average speed, and instantaneous speed are all often referred to simply as “speed” in everyday language. Emphasize the importance in science of using correct terminology to avoid confusion and communicate ideas clearly.

Worked Example: Calculating Average Speed

A marble rolls 5.2 m in 1.8 s. What was the marble’s average speed?

Strategy

We know the distance the marble travels, 5.2 m, and the time interval, 1.8 s. We can use these values in the average speed equation.

Solution

\[ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \frac{5.2\ \text{m}}{1.8\ \text{s}} = 2.9\ \text{m/s} \]

Discussion

Average speed is a scalar, so we do not include direction in the answer. We can check the reasonableness of the answer by estimating: 5 meters divided by 2 seconds is 2.5 m/s. Since 2.5 m/s is close to 2.9 m/s, the answer is reasonable. This is about the speed of a brisk walk, so it also makes sense.

Velocity

The vector version of speed is velocity. Velocity describes the speed and direction of an object. As with speed, it is useful to describe either the average velocity over a time period or the velocity at a specific moment. Average velocity is displacement divided by the time over which the displacement occurs.

\[ \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}} = \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{d}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\boldsymbol{d}_f-\boldsymbol{d}_0}{t_f-t_0} \]

Velocity, like speed, has SI units of meters per second (m/s), but because it is a vector, you must also include a direction. The variable \(\boldsymbol{v}\) for velocity is bold because it is a vector, in contrast to the italic \(v\) for speed, which is a scalar quantity.

Tips for Success

Average speed is not the same thing as average velocity without its direction. As with displacement and distance, changes in direction over a time interval have a larger effect on the relationship between speed and velocity.

Suppose a passenger moved toward the back of a plane with an average velocity of −4 m/s. We cannot tell from the average velocity whether the passenger stopped momentarily or backed up before reaching the back of the plane. To get more details, we must consider smaller segments of the trip over smaller time intervals, such as those shown in Figure 2.9. If we consider infinitesimally small intervals, we can define instantaneous velocity, which is the velocity at a specific instant in time. Instantaneous velocity and average velocity are the same if the velocity is constant.

Distance traveled can differ from the magnitude of displacement. In the same way, speed can differ from the magnitude of velocity. For example, you drive to a store and return home in half an hour. If your car’s odometer shows the total distance traveled was 6 km, then your average speed was 12 km/h. Your average velocity, however, was zero because your displacement for the round trip is zero.

Worked Example: Calculating Average Velocity

A student has a displacement of 304 m north in 180 s. What was the student’s average velocity?

Strategy

We know that the displacement is 304 m north and the time is 180 s. We can use the formula for average velocity to solve the problem.

Solution

\[ \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{d}}{\Delta t} = \frac{304\ \text{m}}{180\ \text{s}} = 1.7\ \text{m/s north} \]

Discussion

Since average velocity is a vector quantity, you must include direction as well as magnitude in the answer. The direction can be omitted until the end to avoid cluttering the problem. Pay attention to significant figures: 304 m has three significant figures, but 180 s has only two, so the quotient should have only two significant figures.

Tips for Success

In this book, \(d\) represents distance and displacement. Similarly, \(v\) represents speed, and \(\boldsymbol{v}\) represents velocity. A variable that is not bold indicates a scalar quantity, while a bold variable indicates a vector quantity. Vectors are sometimes represented by small arrows above variables.

Teacher Support

Use this problem to emphasize the importance of using the correct number of significant figures in calculations. Some students include many digits in their final calculations, incorrectly believing this improves accuracy. Point out that doing this introduces errors. In more complicated calculations, these errors can propagate and cause the final answer to be wrong. Remind students to carry one or two extra digits in intermediate calculations and round the final answer to the correct number of significant figures.

Worked Example: Solving for Displacement when Average Velocity and Time Are Known

Layla jogs with an average velocity of 2.4 m/s east. What is her displacement after 46 seconds?

Strategy

We know that Layla’s average velocity is 2.4 m/s east, and the time interval is 46 seconds. We can rearrange the average velocity formula to solve for the displacement.

Solution

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} &= \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{d}}{\Delta t} \\ \Delta \boldsymbol{d} &= \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}\Delta t \\ &= (2.4\ \text{m/s})(46\ \text{s}) \\ &= 1.1\times10^2\ \text{m east} \end{aligned} \]

Discussion

The answer is about 110 m east, which is a reasonable displacement for slightly less than a minute of jogging. A calculator shows the answer as 110.4 m. We chose to write the answer using scientific notation to make clear that we used only two significant figures.

Tips for Success

Dimensional analysis is a good way to determine whether you solved a problem correctly. Write the calculation using only units to be sure they match on opposite sides of the equal sign. In this example, \(\text{m}=(\text{m/s})(\text{s})\). Seconds cancel, leaving meters.

Worked Example: Solving for Time when Displacement and Average Velocity Are Known

Phillip walks along a straight path from his house to his school. How long will it take him to get to school if he walks 428 m west with an average velocity of 1.7 m/s west?

Strategy

We know that Phillip’s displacement is 428 m west, and his average velocity is 1.7 m/s west. We can calculate the time required for the trip by rearranging the average velocity equation.

Solution

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} &= \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{d}}{\Delta t} \\ \Delta t &= \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{d}}{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}} \\ &= \frac{428\ \text{m}}{1.7\ \text{m/s}} \\ &= 2.5\times10^2\ \text{s} \end{aligned} \]

Discussion

Scientific notation is used because the answer can have only two significant figures. Since time is a scalar, the answer includes only a magnitude and not a direction.

Check Your Understanding

13. Runners’ average velocities

Two runners traveling along the same straight path start and end their run at the same time. At the halfway mark, they have different instantaneous velocities. Is it possible for their average velocities for the entire trip to be the same?

  1. Yes, because average velocity depends on the net or total displacement.
  2. Yes, because average velocity depends on the total distance traveled.
  3. No, because the velocities of both runners must remain exactly the same throughout the journey.
  4. No, because the instantaneous velocities of the runners must remain the same at the midpoint but can vary at other points.

14. Average speed and average velocity

If you divide the total distance traveled on a car trip, as determined by the odometer, by the time for the trip, are you calculating the average speed or the magnitude of the average velocity? Under what circumstances are these two quantities the same?

  1. Average speed. Both are the same when the car is traveling at a constant speed and changing direction.
  2. Average speed. Both are the same when the speed is constant and the car does not change its direction.
  3. Magnitude of average velocity. Both are the same when the car is traveling at a constant speed.
  4. Magnitude of average velocity. Both are the same when the car does not change its direction.

15. Negative average velocity

Is it possible for average velocity to be negative?

  1. Yes, if net displacement is negative.
  2. Yes, if the object’s direction changes during motion.
  3. No, because average velocity describes only the magnitude and not the direction of motion.
  4. No, because average velocity only describes the magnitude in the positive direction of motion.
