Graphing Position as a Function of Time

A graph, like a picture, is worth a thousand words. Graphs not only contain numerical information; they also reveal relationships between physical quantities. In this section, we investigate kinematics by analyzing graphs of position over time.

Graphs have perpendicular axes, one horizontal and the other vertical. When two physical quantities are plotted against each other, the horizontal axis is usually considered the independent variable, and the vertical axis is the dependent variable. In algebra, the horizontal axis is the \(x\)-axis and the vertical axis is the \(y\)-axis. A straight-line graph has the general form:

\[ y = mx + b \]

Here, \(m\) is the slope, defined as the rise divided by the run. The letter \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept: the point at which the line crosses the vertical \(y\)-axis. In a physical situation, these quantities have specific meanings.

In physics, time is usually the independent variable. Other quantities, such as displacement, depend on time. A graph of position versus time therefore has position on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. What do the slope and \(y\)-intercept represent in this case?

Suppose the drive to school is 5 km from home, takes 10 minutes, and your parent drives at a constant velocity the whole time. The position-versus-time graph for this section of the trip would look like the description below.

We call home our origin, \(O\), and start calculating from there, so \(d_0 = 0\). In Figure 2.11, the line also starts at \(d = 0\). This is \(b\) in the equation for a straight line. The initial position in a position-versus-time graph is where the graph crosses the vertical axis at \(t = 0\).

The rise is the change in position (displacement), and the run is the change in time. This relationship can be written:

\[ \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} \tag{2.4} \]

This relationship defines average velocity. Therefore, the slope in a \(d\) versus \(t\) graph is the average velocity.

Tips for Success

Sometimes, such as when graphing both the trip to school and the return trip, the graph behaves differently during different time intervals. If the graph is a series of straight lines, calculate average velocity for each interval by finding the slope. To calculate average velocity for the entire trip, use a weighted average.

Figure 2.12 shows a graph of position versus time for a jet-powered car on a very flat dry lake bed in Nevada.

The slope in Figure 2.12 is average velocity, \(v_{\text{avg}}\), and the intercept is displacement at time zero, \(d_0\). Substituting these symbols into \(y = mx + b\) gives:

\[ d = vt + d_0 \tag{2.5} \]

Or, equivalently:

\[ d = d_0 + vt \tag{2.6} \]

A position-versus-time graph gives a general relationship among displacement, velocity, and time, as well as numerical information about a specific situation. From Figure 2.12, the car is at 400 m at \(t = 0\) s, 650 m at \(t = 1.0\) s, and so on. The graph also tells us about the object's velocity.

Teacher Support: Demonstration

Teacher Demonstration

Help students learn what different graphs of displacement versus time look like.

[Visual] Set up a meter stick.

  1. If you can find a remote-control car, have one student record times as you send the car forward along the stick, then backward, then forward again at constant velocity.
  2. Put the recorded times and changes in position together.
  3. Have students coach you to draw a position-versus-time graph.

Each leg of the journey should be a straight line with a different slope. The parts where the car is going forward should have a positive slope. The part where it is going backward should have a negative slope.

[OL] Ask whether the place taken as zero affects the graph.

[AL] Is it realistic to draw a position graph that starts at rest without some curve in it? Why might we be able to neglect the curve in some scenarios?

[All] Discuss what can be learned from the graph. Students should be able to read the net displacement, and they can also use the graph to determine total distance traveled. Ask how speed or velocity is reflected in the graph. Emphasize that the steepness of the line (slope) measures speed and that the direction of the slope indicates direction of motion.

[AL] Some students might recognize that a curve in the line represents a changing slope, a preview of acceleration.

Snap Lab: Graphing Motion

In this activity, you will release a ball down a ramp and graph the ball’s displacement versus time.

  • Choose an open location with lots of space to spread out so there is less chance of tripping or falling due to rolling balls.

Materials

  • 1 ball
  • 1 board
  • 2 or 3 books
  • 1 stopwatch
  • 1 tape measure
  • 6 pieces of masking tape
  • 1 piece of graph paper
  • 1 pencil

Procedure

  1. Build a ramp by placing one end of the board on top of the stack of books. Adjust the location as necessary until there is no obstacle along the straight path from the bottom of the ramp for at least the next 3 m.
  2. Mark distances of 0.5 m, 1.0 m, 1.5 m, 2.0 m, 2.5 m, and 3.0 m from the bottom of the ramp. Write the distances on the tape.
  3. Have one person act as the experimenter and release the ball from the top of the ramp. If the ball does not reach the 3.0 m mark, increase the incline by adding another book. Repeat as necessary.
  4. Have the experimenter release the ball. A second person, the timer, begins timing when the ball reaches the bottom of the ramp and stops timing when the ball reaches 0.5 m. A third person, the recorder, records the time in a data table.
  5. Repeat Step 4, stopping the time at distances of 1.0 m, 1.5 m, 2.0 m, 2.5 m, and 3.0 m from the bottom of the ramp.
  6. Use your time and displacement measurements to make a position-versus-time graph of the ball’s motion.
  7. Repeat Steps 4 through 6 with different people acting as experimenter, timer, and recorder. Do you get the same measurements regardless of who releases the ball, measures the time, or records the result? Discuss possible causes of any discrepancies.

True or False: The average speed of the ball will be less than the average velocity of the ball.

  1. True
  2. False

Teacher Support: Snap Lab

[BL][OL] Emphasize that the motion in this lab is the motion of the ball as it rolls along the floor. Ask students where zero should be.

[AL] Ask students what the graph would look like if they began timing at the top rather than the bottom of the ramp. Why would the graph look different? What might account for the difference?

[BL][OL] Have students compare graphs made with different people taking different roles. Ask them to determine and compare average speeds for each interval. What were the absolute differences in speeds, and what were the percent differences? Do the differences appear random or systematic? Why might there be systematic differences between sets of measurements made by different people?

[BL][OL] Repeat the comparison of graphs and interval speeds. Ask students to discuss absolute and percent differences, whether differences are random or systematic, and why changing the people in each role might produce systematic differences.

Solving Problems Using Position vs. Time Graphs

How do we use graphs to solve for quantities such as velocity?

Worked Example: Calculate Average Velocity for a Jet Car

Find the average velocity of the car whose position is graphed in Figure 2.12.

Strategy

The slope of a graph of \(d\) versus \(t\) is average velocity, since slope equals rise over run:

\[ \text{slope} = \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} = v_{\text{avg}} \tag{2.7} \]

Since the slope is constant here, any two points on the graph can be used to find it.

Solution

  1. Choose two points on the line. Here, use \((6.4\ \text{s}, 2000\ \text{m})\) and \((0.50\ \text{s}, 525\ \text{m})\). Any two points on the line could be used.
  2. Substitute the values into the equation. When calculating a change (\(\Delta\)), always use final value minus initial value.
\[ \begin{aligned} v_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t} \\ &= \frac{2000\ \text{m} - 525\ \text{m}} {6.4\ \text{s} - 0.50\ \text{s}} \\ &= 250\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned} \tag{2.8} \]

Discussion

This is an impressively high land speed (900 km/h, or about 560 mi/h): much greater than the typical highway speed limit of 27 m/s or 96 km/h, but considerably shy of the record of 343 m/s or 1,234 km/h, set in 1997.

Teacher Support

If the graph of position is a straight line, students need only the slope of the line (rise divided by run) to calculate average velocity. They can use whichever points on the line are most convenient.

What if the position graph is more complicated than a straight line? What if the object speeds up, or turns around and goes backward? Can we determine anything about its velocity from a graph of that motion?

Figure 2.13 shows a jet-powered car getting up to speed after starting at rest. Time starts at zero, as if measured with a stopwatch. The displacement and velocity are initially 200 m and 15 m/s, respectively.

The graph in Figure 2.13 is curved rather than straight. Its slope becomes steeper as time progresses, showing that velocity is increasing. The slope at any point on a position-versus-time graph is the instantaneous velocity at that point. Find it by drawing a straight line tangent to the curve at the point of interest and calculating the tangent line's slope. The average velocity is the net displacement divided by the time traveled.

Worked Example: Instantaneous Velocity for a Jet Car

Calculate the instantaneous velocity of the jet car at \(t=25\ \text{s}\) by finding the slope of the tangent line at point Q in Figure 2.13.

Strategy

The slope of a curve at a point is equal to the slope of a straight line tangent to the curve at that point.

Solution

  1. Find the tangent line to the curve at \(t=25\ \text{s}\).
  2. Determine the endpoints of the tangent. They correspond to a position of 1,300 m at \(t=19\ \text{s}\) and a position of 3,120 m at \(t=32\ \text{s}\).
  3. Substitute the endpoints into the slope equation to solve for velocity.
\[ \begin{aligned} v_Q &= \frac{\Delta d_Q}{\Delta t_Q} \\ &= \frac{(3120 - 1300)\ \text{m}} {(32 - 19)\ \text{s}} \\ &= \frac{1820\ \text{m}}{13\ \text{s}} \\ &= 140\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned} \tag{2.9} \]

Discussion

The entire graph of \(v\) versus \(t\) can be obtained in this fashion.

Teacher Support: Tangent Lines

A tangent is a line that touches a curve at one point. Explain that the slope of this line gives the instantaneous velocity. Students can use the tangent's slope to tell whether velocity is increasing, decreasing, positive, negative, or zero. At this level, students can estimate the tangent by eye.

[AL] You could find the instantaneous velocity at each point along the graph. If you graphed those values, you would have a graph of velocity.
